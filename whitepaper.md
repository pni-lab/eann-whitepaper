## Executive summary

Brains never stop learning. A new face, a new route home or a new word is added to what we already know without wiping it out, while the brain keeps track of how certain it is, all on about 20 watts. Current AI, for all its staggering progress, still struggles with exactly these capacities: large models are trained offline, tend to overwrite old knowledge when they learn something new, represent uncertainty poorly and consume a great deal of energy. Neuroscience has catalogued many of the mechanisms behind the brain's abilities, from synaptic plasticity to replay and attractor dynamics, but they are not yet tied together by a principle that explains why they work and how they carry across scales.

This white paper sets out a research programme built on one such framework. Its central hypothesis is that inference and learning rest on elementary physical principles, which brains did not invent but came to exploit ever better. The programme gives this hypothesis a concrete form: a specific family of models, *entropic attractor networks* (eANNs), together with a formal theory of why they infer and learn.

*The model family.* eANNs are stochastic attractor networks of simple units. Each unit infers by combining its own internal "bias" with weighted evidence from the other units, and each coupling learns, locally, by contrasting the correlations it observes with those the network predicts (a predictive-coding rule). From these two rules alone, the networks store what they learn as attractors that act as Bayesian priors, drive these attractors towards orthogonality even for correlated inputs, learn sequences through asymmetric couplings, generalize to new inputs and resist catastrophic forgetting {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`. Four features set the family apart:

- **Derived, not designed.** The inference and learning rules follow from first principles rather than being chosen for performance, and Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines appear as special cases.
- **Concrete and plausible.** The rules are local, the noise is functional (it encodes precision), and the parameters are few and interpretable: biases and couplings, from which the attractors can be read off directly. All of this is within reach of biological circuits and of emerging physical hardware.
- **One model, two uses.** The same network is a generative model of large-scale brain dynamics that can be fitted to fMRI, and a trainable stochastic learner, so results in one domain carry over to the other.
- **Scale-agnostic and testable.** The form of the construction is expected to survive coarse-graining ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)), so models of one family may describe single cells, circuits and whole brain regions alike. Its signatures, such as priors stored as attractors, approximately orthogonal attractors and noise that tracks precision, are concrete predictions, each with a stated criterion for failure ([Section 7](#sec-7)).

*First evidence.* The programme does not start from zero ([Section 2](#sec-2)). Besides the analytical and simulation results {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`, we have found first evidence that human functional brain networks display the key signature of the eANN model family: approximately orthogonal attractors. We have also found that these attractors correspond closely to canonical large-scale brain networks, replicate across datasets, and capture task-related changes and altered dynamics in clinical conditions {cite:p}`10.7554/eLife.98725`, and independent groups have started to apply these networks {cite:p}`10.3389/fnins.2026.1948718`. A connectome-based Hopfield-type model of mouse cortex, developed separately, likewise predicts fMRI co-activation patterns from its attractor landscape {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013995`. These results are early, but they show that the networks exist, work and can be fitted to real brains.

*From matter to units.* Our published derivation {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472` starts from the free energy principle applied to a network of nested subsystems. The natural next question is which physical conditions a piece of matter must meet to behave as one of its units. We have answered it in a derivation that will be published shortly (as of v0.9.2 of this document): the minimal form of the free energy principle that the units of eANNs require follows from elementary, physically plausible assumptions about a single unit ([Section 3](#sec-3) gives a high-level summary; detailed manuscript in preparation). The theory is stated at the level of the information geometry of physically plausible dynamical systems and of their slow, coarse observables, given an appropriate separation of timescales. Under these assumptions, the familiar ingredients of Bayesian brain theories appear as consequences rather than postulates. Normalizing a perturbed physical ensemble turns it into a prior, a likelihood and a model evidence. A prediction error turns out to be the only kind of change that preserves normalization. The unit's sigmoid response function follows from its bounded range, and its fluctuations encode the precision of what it represents. Perception becomes fast relaxation along information-geometric fibres, and learning slow relaxation over the base manifold that matches predicted to observed correlations ([Figure 4](#fig-unit)). This gives a formal, constructive route from physical properties of matter to networks that store their priors as attractors and learn with a local rule.

The programme is organized around three hypotheses, each tested in one line of work:

1. **Matter can infer and learn (Line A, "Approach and theory").** In their most elementary form, inference and learning are capacities of suitably organized matter rather than algorithms imposed on it. Line A completes the unit-level theory and extends it to hierarchy, action, planning and thermodynamic cost.
2. **Biology exploits this across scales (Line B, "Brains").** Living systems use this elementary form of inference at many scales and show measurable signatures of it. Line B fits eANNs to fMRI data as generative models of large-scale brain dynamics, tests the theory's predictions, and asks which computations are carried by large-scale attractors and which by processes below them, using learning, extinction, expectations and pain as test cases.
3. **Machines can do the same, natively (Line C, "Computers").** Line C develops eANNs as an alternative route to AI, tests them on a neuroscience-inspired benchmark battery, and explores implementations on emerging thermodynamic hardware, where noise is a resource rather than a nuisance.

The programme does not promise a full account of the brain's complexity, but a firmer grasp of its roots: (A) predictions derived from one construction that should hold, in brains and machines alike, at different scales; (B) generative models of large-scale brain dynamics whose components have a clear computational meaning; and (C) learning machines in which continual learning and uncertainty are built in rather than added on. Because the three lines test one construction from different sides, each sharpens the others: the theory tells experiments what to look for, brain data show which of its forms biology uses, and machines test it under full control. The potential payoff is correspondingly broad. Neuroscience would gain a principle that connects its scales, and AI a route to brain-like systems that learn continually and represent their own uncertainty, potentially at a fraction of today's energy cost. If inference and learning are indeed capacities of suitably organized matter, understanding brains and building intelligent machines become, at their roots, one scientific problem.

## 1. Introduction

(sec-1-1)=
### 1.1 What brains do and machines still struggle with

Modern AI writes, reasons, generates images and delivers rigorous mathematical proofs, but its progress towards biological intelligence is strikingly uneven. Large models are trained offline, on vast data, with global loss functions and with methods shaped by the constraints of von Neumann hardware (the "hardware lottery", {cite:t}`10.1145/3467017`). At least four capacities that brains show routinely remain hard for them.

The first is *continual (or test-time) learning*. In brains, learning and inference are inseparable and never stop, whereas artificial networks trained on new data tend to overwrite what they knew (catastrophic forgetting). The same limitation shows up within a single long task: in a recent comparison, language-model agents given a day of compute fell behind the gains that repeated sampling would predict, whereas expert humans kept improving at least over two weeks {cite:p}`mang2026`, and probably for far longer. From the perspective developed below, this is less a matter of protecting old weights than of missing timescales: inference runs, but there is no slower level of structure that keeps adapting while the task is performed, and no level slower still that consolidates what was learned (Sections [3](#sec-3), [4.3](#sec-4-3) and [5](#sec-5)). The second is *uncertainty*: brains act on calibrated uncertainty {cite:p}`10.1016/j.tins.2004.10.007; 10.1038/nn.4240`, while most artificial networks represent it poorly {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.1706.04599` or only through add-on machinery {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.1506.02142; 10.48550/arXiv.1612.01474`. The third is *frugality*. Brains learn with local plasticity driven by noisy signals, on a tiny energy budget {cite:p}`10.1097/00004647-200110000-00001`, whereas backpropagation needs globally orchestrated error transport {cite:p}`10.1038/s41583-020-0277-3` and runs on deterministic hardware that spends much of its energy suppressing noise {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.1906.02243; 10.1145/3467017`. The fourth is *genuine agency*: brains plan and act on an internal model of the world. In current AI, despite impressive progress, agency and active, goal-directed behaviour may still be largely mimicry {cite:p}`10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8; 10.1016/j.tics.2023.10.002`, or behaviour engineered on top of a pretrained model by reinforcement learning {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.2203.02155`, rather than something that arises from the model itself {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.2402.01817`.

Brains show that all of this is physically achievable, and neuroscience has identified many of the mechanisms involved, from local plasticity {cite:p}`10.1523/jneurosci.18-24-10464.1998` and replay {cite:p}`10.1126/science.8036517` to attractor dynamics {cite:p}`10.1038/s41583-022-00642-0`. What AI lacks is therefore not an existence proof but a principled foundation: an account of why these mechanisms work that is general enough to carry over to other substrates.

(sec-1-2)=
### 1.2 Lost across scales

Few objects in science have been described at as many scales as the brain: molecules and synapses, neurons and circuits, regions and whole-brain networks, each with its own methods and its own models. What is still missing is an agreed and testable account, at the level of physics, of how living matter can infer and learn at all, and why similar capacities recur at very different scales, from single cells to whole brains {cite:p}`10.1007/s10339-005-0016-8; 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02688`.

One symptom is that models at different scales rarely connect. Bottom-up approaches build whole-brain models from local biophysics, with neural masses, neural fields or mean-field approximations. They are mechanistic, but every modelling choice multiplies the number of plausible variants, and macro-scale data alone can rarely decide between them. Top-down approaches describe macro-scale phenomena, such as functional connectivity and resting-state networks, without a mechanism that generates them. The two research cultures rarely meet {cite:p}`10.1038/s41583-025-00946-x`. A principle that holds, or is at least approximated, at every scale would give both a common reference, against which bottom-up mechanisms and top-down descriptions could be tested.

(sec-1-3)=
### 1.3 Why existing frameworks do not close the gap on their own

Several frameworks supply parts of such a principle. None, we argue, supplies all of it, and the approach proposed here is meant to complement them rather than replace them ([Section 10](#sec-10) compares them in more detail).

- **Biophysical whole-brain models** are mechanistic, interpretable and the most direct link between large-scale dynamics and physiology {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000092; 10.3389/fninf.2013.00010; 10.1038/nn.4497`. But they are not designed to learn or to perform the functions whose neural basis they describe, which limits what they can offer AI, and they take the local mechanism as given at one privileged scale. We aim to add a scale-agnostic perspective ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)).
- **Task-optimized deep networks ("neuroconnectionism")** have given neuroscience its most successful models of sensory representations, and a productive way of comparing brains and machines on the same tasks {cite:p}`10.1038/s41593-019-0520-2; 10.1038/s41583-023-00705-w`. Their strength is task fidelity. Their neurobiological validity is partial, however (learning by backpropagation, idealized units, rarely generative models of whole-brain dynamics), and architectures, objectives and learning rules are chosen heuristically and by fit to data rather than derived from principles. They show which functions a brain-like system can learn, but not why suitably organized matter comes to infer and learn in the first place.
- **Energy-based associative memories**, from Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.79.8.2554; 10.1207/s15516709cog0901_7` to dense associative memories with very large capacity and a close link to attention in transformers {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.1606.01164; 10.48550/arXiv.2008.06996; 10.48550/arXiv.2008.02217`, show how far memory and computation can go with local interactions. Their energy function and learning rule are chosen by the designer, typically for capacity, and few are tied to brain data. The networks proposed here recover them as special cases for binary units, symmetric couplings and equilibrium ([Section 3.4](#sec-3-4)), but derive their energy function and learning rule instead of choosing them.
- **Physical learning and neuromorphic computing** move learning into the substrate itself: equilibrium propagation and coupled learning {cite:p}`10.3389/fncom.2017.00024; 10.1103/physrevx.11.021045`, classical and neural cellular automata {cite:p}`10.1016/0167-2789(84)90245-8; 10.23915/distill.00023`, and spiking networks {cite:p}`10.1016/S0893-6080(97)00011-7` with rules such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity {cite:p}`10.1523/jneurosci.18-24-10464.1998`, which promise brain-like energy efficiency on neuromorphic hardware {cite:p}`10.1109/JPROC.2014.2304638; 10.1109/MM.2018.112130359; 10.1038/s41586-019-1677-2; 10.1038/s42254-020-0208-2`. Their learning rules, however, are mostly designed by hand or approximate backpropagation {cite:p}`10.1109/MSP.2019.2931595; 10.1038/s41467-020-17236-y`. Work that derives spike-based plasticity from likelihood or expectation maximization {cite:p}`10.1162/neco.2006.18.6.1318; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003037` and reads spikes as samples from a posterior {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002211` comes closest to the inferential reading adopted here ([Section 4.4](#sec-4-4)), and even the circuit for gradient-based credit assignment can self-assemble under uniform local plasticity {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.2606245123`. In all of these, however, the objective or the plasticity rule is postulated rather than derived from the physics of the unit.
- **Large-scale attractor and energy-landscape models of brain dynamics**, in a tradition that goes back to {cite:t}`10.1017/cbo9780511623257`, include multistable and ghost-attractor regimes of connectome-based mean-field models {cite:p}`10.1523/jneurosci.2523-11.2012; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004644`, coupled-oscillator models {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neuroimage.2011.04.010`, maximum-entropy (Ising-type) landscapes fitted to fMRI {cite:p}`10.1038/ncomms2388; 10.1098/rsta.2016.0287; 10.1038/s41598-018-20123-8`, Hopfield-type networks built from mouse cortical connectivity {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013995` or estimated from MEG {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2023.11.027`, attractor-state perspectives on the default mode network {cite:p}`10.1016/j.cobeha.2025.101546`, and our own connectome-based attractor networks of the human brain {cite:p}`10.7554/eLife.98725`, now also applied by independent groups {cite:p}`10.3389/fnins.2026.1948718`. They capture real features of brain dynamics, but most are phenomenological: couplings are taken from connectivity or fitted to data, and dynamics and storage rules are chosen rather than derived. This holds even where the landscape is fitted directly to regional time series. A recent study relates the geometry of such a landscape to attention {cite:p}`10.1038/s41467-026-69041-8`, but the form of its dynamical model is again chosen, so the attractors depend on modelling and fitting choices that the data alone constrain poorly. Why brain dynamics take this form, what the attractors compute, and how such landscapes could serve AI as trainable learners remain largely open.
- **The free energy principle (FEP) and active inference** offer the broadest first-principles account of self-organization as inference {cite:p}`10.1038/nrn2787; 10.1016/j.physrep.2023.07.001`. Their route is top-down: from a whole system that persists, separated from its environment by a Markov blanket, to internal states that can be read as encoding beliefs about external ones. As a principle, the FEP is tested through the process theories built on it, such as predictive coding and active inference, and recent work has specified the conditions under which its inferential reading holds {cite:p}`10.3390/e23030293; 10.1016/j.plrev.2021.11.001; 10.1017/s0140525x21002351`. The present programme approaches it from the bottom up. From a handful of explicit physical assumptions about a single unit ([Section 3](#sec-3)), it derives, we think plausibly, the minimal form of the FEP that elementary units of inference and learning require (manuscript in preparation), and in this setting the principle and its Bayesian readings hold rigorously. Everything beyond this minimal form, from structured priors to sequence learning, comes from composing such units into attractor networks ([Section 4](#sec-4)), as described by {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`. The FEP is thus a guiding principle, an interim result derived at the level of the unit, and a link to Bayesian brain theories and active inference. What the construction adds is a concrete theory with falsifiable predictions.

(sec-1-4)=
### 1.4 What is missing: a substrate-independent account of inference and learning across scales

Taken together, Sections [1.1](#sec-1-1) to [1.3](#sec-1-3) point to one gap. What is missing is not another model of a particular phenomenon at a particular scale, but a substrate-independent, that is, geometric, account of how collective dynamics with separated timescales organize into increasingly complex forms of inference, learning and action. Such an account would describe a physical candidate phenomenon for cognition: one that evolution did not have to invent, only to exploit ({cite:t}`10.1002/bies.201900228; 10.1007/s00018-023-04790-z`; see also {cite:t}`10.1093/nc/niab013`, {cite:t}`10.1016/j.pbiomolbio.2022.05.006`), and one that may offer guidance towards brain-like artificial cognition. Our approach breaks this challenge down into two parts: minimal units, and their composition ([Figure 1](#fig-gap)).

The first part is the elementary building block, the account that [Section 1.2](#sec-1-2) found missing: how a piece of matter can infer and learn at all. Rather than postulating inference, or establishing it for whole systems under conditions that are hard to verify ([Section 1.3](#sec-1-3)), it calls for a derivation from assumptions about a single unit that can be checked for a given substrate, and that can fail. It also has to be stated in terms that do not depend on any particular substrate. We use the information geometry of a system's slow, coarse observables, in which the same adaptive, self-aligning structure can be recognized in different substrates, implementations and scales: in molecular networks and synapses, in neurons and circuits, in brain regions, and in engineered hardware ([Section 3](#sec-3)).

The second part is composition: how such units, coupled together, give rise to new computational capacities at the next spatial and temporal scale, and whether the collective is again a unit of the same kind. This is where the gaps of [Section 1.1](#sec-1-1) belong. Continual learning, calibrated uncertainty, frugal local learning and agency are not properties of single units. If they arise from general units, they must arise from the collective dynamics: from the structure the units build together, and from the slower timescales on which that structure changes. Networks built in this way already show several of these properties, among them priors stored as attractors, resistance to catastrophic forgetting and sequence learning ([Section 2](#sec-2)). What has been missing is a formal account of why composition produces them, how far it carries across scales, and which of the capacities of [Section 1.1](#sec-1-1) it can deliver (Sections [4](#sec-4) and [5](#sec-5)).

Both parts also have to build on the strengths of the frameworks reviewed in [Section 1.3](#sec-1-3), which the new account is meant to complement and connect rather than replace. Its models should work as learners, as task-optimized networks do. They should be fitted to brain data, as biophysical and landscape models are. Their rules should follow from principles rather than from choice, as the FEP demands. And they should be implementable in physical substrates, as physical and neuromorphic learning aims to be. Each of these frameworks already delivers on its own requirement; what a common construction can add is a way to meet them together.

This is a programme about roots, not about the whole tree. The brain's many cell types, its molecular machinery, its developmental history and its individual variability will remain a challenge for a long time. What we aim for is a much better understanding of the roots from which this complexity grows, and which each biological implementation realizes in its own way. The rest of this document describes how far our approach has taken both parts, and what remains to be done.

### 1.5 How to read this document

*Two routes through the same material.* The argument has a conceptual order and a chronological one, and this document uses both. Conceptually, it runs from the unit to the network: a single unit infers and learns under stated physical assumptions, such units compose into networks, and these networks are then fitted to brains and built into machines. This is the order of the executive summary. Chronologically, the networks came first {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`. They were derived from the free energy principle, simulated and fitted to human brain data, and the unit-level construction was developed afterwards to ground them in physics, which turned variational free energy minimization from an assumption into a derived result. The main text follows this chronological order. It starts from what is published ([Section 2](#sec-2)), then turns to the newer foundation underneath it ([Section 3](#sec-3), an outline of unpublished results), and the two routes meet in [Section 4](#sec-4), where the same networks are obtained again, this time from below (a recap of Spisak & Friston, 2026).

After these three sections, [Section 5](#sec-5) asks what attractors are, and [Section 6](#sec-6) collects biology-like phenomena that may follow. [Section 7](#sec-7) lists the predictions with their failure criteria, [Section 8](#sec-8) the technical plan, and Sections [9](#sec-9) and [10](#sec-10) the risks and the relation to other approaches. [Section 11](#sec-11) collects further-reaching ideas on which nothing else depends. ## 2. Where we stand: what is already established

The programme does not start from zero. Two peer-reviewed papers form its published core, one theoretical and one empirical, and they show that the networks at the centre of this white paper ([Figure 2](#fig-rules)) exist, work and can be fitted to human brain data. In the logical order of the argument these networks are the end point; historically, they came first ([Section 1.5](#sec-1-5)).

*In theory and simulation* {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`. {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472` derived a class of stochastic attractor networks by applying the FEP recursively to a network of nested subsystems, without hand-designing either the inference or the learning rule ([Figure 2A](#fig-rules)). The FEP was an assumption at this stage; [Section 3](#sec-3) derives its minimal form from the physics of a single unit. Each node infers by combining its own bias with weighted evidence from the other nodes, and each coupling learns by contrasting observed with predicted correlations, a local, predictive-coding rule. The paper showed, analytically and in simulations, that the attractors of such networks act as Bayesian priors ([Figure 2C](#fig-rules)). Additionally, we found that the learning rule does something that was never put into it: even when the inputs are strongly correlated, it drives the attractors towards approximate orthogonality ([Figure 2B](#fig-rules)). The networks find a decorrelated, near-orthogonal code on their own, the kind of representation that efficient-coding theories have long proposed for the brain, and that machine learning usually has to enforce with additional objectives or constraints. Orthogonal attractors interfere least with one another, which fits two further findings: the networks generalize to new inputs, and they resist catastrophic forgetting. Sequential input, finally, produces asymmetric couplings and directed sequence dynamics ([Figure 2D](#fig-rules)). The simulation code is available at: <https://github.com/pni-lab/fep-attractor-network>.

*In the human brain* {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`. {cite:t}`10.7554/eLife.98725` built networks of this class directly from human functional connectivity. Their attractors were approximately orthogonal, held up against strict null models, and provided initial evidence that the landscape corresponds to canonical large-scale brain networks, replicates across datasets, and captures task-related changes and altered dynamics in clinical conditions. Applying the networks at the scale of brain regions relies on the scale-agnostic nature of the framework: the construction is expected to keep its form under coarse-graining ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)), so that brain regions, and the slow signals that fMRI resolves in space and time, can be modelled as units of the same family. This property is currently a well-motivated heuristic, and formalizing it is a goal of Line A. The networks have also begun to be used outside our group: in a fully independent study, {cite:t}`10.3389/fnins.2026.1948718` applied them, with the pretrained embedding of Englert et al. (2026), to resting-state fMRI of individuals with high smartphone addiction scores. The attractor states supplied the reference frame (internal versus external context) around which the study's clearest, multiple-comparison-corrected group differences emerged: altered transitions between the default mode and attention networks. Beyond our framework, a connectome-based Hopfield-type model of mouse cortex predicts fMRI co-activation patterns from its attractor landscape {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013995`, and a recurrent Hopfield network with asymmetric couplings, fitted to MEG, estimates meso-scale effective connectivity and predicts brain dynamics {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2023.11.027`. ## 3. A minimal inferring and learning unit

[Section 2](#sec-2) started from the networks. This section goes one level down, to the single unit from which they can be built: it states the construction of the minimal unit and what follows from it. The aim is a sufficient route from physics to inference and learning, not a necessary one: a minimal and physically plausible model of how matter can be organized to infer and learn. The construction also changes the status of the free energy principle. In [Section 2](#sec-2), the FEP was the starting point from which the networks were derived. Here, its minimal form, a single unit that minimizes variational free energy, follows from the unit's physics. The FEP thus turns from an assertion into an important interim result, and the networks of [Section 2](#sec-2) come to rest on stated physical assumptions rather than on the principle itself ([Section 4](#sec-4)). The full derivations are in the manuscript "From physical dynamics to perception and learning" (Spisak et al., in preparation; available on request) {raw:latex}`\bPrep{`{span .tag .tag-in-preparation}`In preparation`{raw:latex}`}`. Here we give the assumptions, the results in informal form, and the known cases they reduce to. The notation, the ordering of timescales and a map of which result uses which assumption are collected in *Setting, notation and the assumption–result map*.

(sec-3-1)=
### 3.1 Why this is possible now

That inference might be a property of matter, and not only of minds, is an old intuition. Several developments, some long established and some recent, now fit together and make it possible to turn it into a construction.

*A geometry in which prediction errors are natural objects.* The non-parametric exponential manifold of {cite:t}`10.1214/aos/1176324311` describes the space of all normalized probability densities of a system, without fixing a parametric model in advance. On this manifold every tangent vector is centred: it has the form "actual minus expected". Specifically, writing a density in exponential coordinates, {math}`p = e^{u-\psi(u)}\,p_0` with normalizer {math}`\psi(u) = \log \mathbb{E}_{p_0}[e^{u}]`, normalization alone gives

```{math}
\partial \log p \;=\; \partial u - \partial\psi \;=\; \partial u - \mathbb{E}_p[\partial u], \qquad \text{since} \qquad \mathbb{E}_p[\partial \log p] = \int \partial p = \partial \!\int p = 0 .
```

Prediction errors, which Bayesian brain theories postulate, can therefore be read as the generic way a normalized ensemble changes.

*Learning as constrained maximum entropy.* Jaynes' maximum-entropy principle {cite:p}`10.1103/physrev.106.620` and the duality theory of exponential families {cite:p}`10.1007/978-4-431-55978-8` relate each parameter of a distribution to the expectation it controls. Recent work has made the correspondence between constrained maximum entropy and the FEP explicit {cite:p}`10.1098/rsfs.2022.0029; 10.48550/arXiv.2204.11900`. This is what allows slow structural coordinates, such as couplings, to be treated as thermodynamic conjugates of correlations.

*Timescale separation as a tool.* Averaging and adiabatic elimination {cite:p}`pavliotis2008; 10.1007/978-3-642-88338-5` turn a separation of timescales into a precise statement about which variables can be treated as fixed, which as equilibrated, and which as slowly drifting. In the construction this separates perception from learning.

*A sharper question.* The critical debate about the FEP {cite:p}`10.3390/e23030293; 10.1016/j.plrev.2021.11.001; 10.1017/s0140525x21002351` has clarified what a physical account of inference must specify. A construction whose assumptions are local and explicit is a well-posed contribution to that debate, rather than a rival grand theory.

*A known target.* Finally, the network rules of {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472` are published and a construction from below has to recover them, including the exact form of the learning rule and its timing. Until recently, there was no comparably concrete target against which a unit-level construction could be checked.

(sec-3-2)=
### 3.2 Assumptions

The construction ([Figure 4](#fig-unit)) uses a small set of explicit assumptions, chosen by Occam's razor: whenever several structures could support a result, we take the least elaborate one.

1. **A normalized reference ensemble (A1).** The stationary densities of the system are positive and regular enough to admit local exponential coordinates, and environmental forcing acts as an exponential tilt of a reference state.
2. **Coarse observables with a double separation of timescales (A2).** Microscopic variables relax fastest, retained mesoscopic coordinates next, and slow structural coordinates (couplings) much more slowly. The environment varies on an intermediate timescale.
3. **Bounded coordinates and a point of view (A3).** Retained coordinates are bounded, as in any finite physical system. One of them, the exposed coordinate, is followed from its own point of view, and the others form its boundary. No Markov blanket is required: shielding from the environment is a special case, not a precondition.
4. **Minimal interaction and least-structured response (A4).** Near a generic operating point, only the leading interaction between boundary and exposed coordinate is kept, and the resting law of the exposed coordinate is the maximum-entropy law on its range.
5. **Causal renewal, for networks (A5).** Coupled units renew together, each forming its prediction from the preceding state of its boundary.

Each assumption is an empirical question for a given substrate, and each can fail.

(sec-3-3)=
### 3.3 Results

The results are stated informally; precise statements, conditions and proofs are given in the manuscript in preparation. For each result we name the assumptions it uses and, in one line, the route by which it is obtained.

- **R1. Bayesian structure from normalization** (A1, A2). Normalizing the tilted ensemble over fast and slow coordinates turns the reference state into a prior, the tilt into a likelihood and the normalizer into model evidence. No beliefs are assigned to microstates. *Route:* the normalization of an exponentially tilted density, read at fixed slow coordinates.
- **R2. Prediction error as a tangent** (A1–A3). Along the fast direction, the centred tangent of the exposed coordinate is its prediction error, ε = μ − E\[μ | s, W\]. It follows from preserving normalization alone. *Route:* the tangent space of the non-parametric exponential manifold.
- **R3. The response function from boundedness** (A1, A3, A4). The unit's mean response is the derivative of the log-partition function of the bounded maximum-entropy family: the Langevin function L(x) = coth x − 1/x, which reduces to tanh for a binary coordinate. No activation function is chosen by hand. *Route:* Legendre duality of the bounded maximum-entropy family.
- **R4. Perception as fast relaxation** (A1–A4). The fast relaxation of the mesoscopic coordinates moves the distribution of the unit's state towards the posterior; it is descent on variational free energy at fixed structure. *Route:* adiabatic elimination of the microscopic variables.
- **R5. Learning as slow relaxation** (A1–A4; timing from A5). Timescale separation makes the expected correlations of the mesoscopic coordinates the Legendre duals of the slow structural coordinates. The structure then climbs the average evidence, driven by a contrastive learning signal: observed minus expected correlation, a Hebbian minus an anti-Hebbian term. *Route:* a dynamical form of Jaynes' constrained maximum entropy, by averaging over the fast timescale.
- **R6. Uncertainty from fluctuations** (A1–A4). At both timescales, fluctuations sample the corresponding posterior. For the fast state, variance equals gain, a fluctuation–dissipation relation; for the slow structure, this is the idea of synaptic sampling {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004485`. *Route:* the second derivative of the log-partition function, which is both a variance and a susceptibility.

The two centred quantities that drive perception and learning can be written compactly:

:::{admonition} The construction in two sentences
:class: note

*Perception.* A prediction error, geometrically, is nothing more than a normalization-preserving, and therefore expectation-centred, tangent vector on a non-parametric statistical manifold.

```{math}
\varepsilon_\mu = \underbrace{\mu}_{\text{actual state}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\mu \mid s, W]}_{\text{expected state (prediction)}}
```

*Learning.* A synapse is the thermodynamic Legendre conjugate of a correlation.

```{math}
\psi(W) = \sup_{q}\Big\{ \underbrace{W}_{\text{structural parameter}}\, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[s\,\mu]}_{\text{expected correlation}} - \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, q_0)}_{\text{relative entropy}} \Big\}
```

```{math}
\varepsilon_W = \underbrace{s\,\mu}_{\text{observed correlation}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[s\,\mu \mid W]}_{\text{expected correlation}}
```

*Here ψ is the log-partition function (a free energy) and q₀ the reference law of the unconstrained dynamics. The structural parameter and the expected correlation form a Legendre pair, with relative entropy as the dual potential. Since ∂ψ/∂W = E\[sμ | W\], the learning error is the gradient of the dual objective W·sμ − ψ(W): learning climbs it until expected and observed correlations agree, which is Jaynes' constrained maximum entropy in dynamical form. Synapse is meant in the general sense: any slow coupling between units.*
:::

(sec-3-4)=
### 3.4 Known cases, and what the unit does not do

A new construction should reduce to known results where those apply. For binary units, symmetric couplings and equilibrium, R3 gives the familiar tanh response of Ising-type models, and R5 gives a contrastive, Hebbian minus anti-Hebbian rule of the same form as Boltzmann-machine learning {cite:p}`10.1207/s15516709cog0901_7`. For a continuous bounded coordinate, R3 gives the Langevin function of the classical paramagnet. R6 is the fluctuation–dissipation relation of the exponential family {cite:p}`10.1088/0034-4885/29/1/306`. What is new is not these ingredients but their joint derivation from one set of physical assumptions, their Bayesian reading, and the timing of the learning signal. In the construction this timing follows from causal renewal (A5), and in the networks of {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472` it is predicted to be what makes the attractors orthogonalize (P3).

Just as important is what the construction does not claim. It shows one route from physics to inference and learning, not the only one, and it does not show that brains, or any particular material, take this route. Where its assumptions fail, for example without a clear separation of timescales, the inferential reading is not guaranteed, and the learning signal becomes biased.

The construction also defines a *family* of units and networks, not one set of equations. Three ingredients come from the substrate and select a member of the family: the reference measure of the microstates, the bounds of the retained coordinates (which fix the response function, the Langevin function in general and tanh only for a binary coordinate), and the type of intrinsic interaction between mesoscopic coordinates. What all members share is the Bayesian structure, the centred prediction error, the Legendre-dual learning signal and the signatures that follow from them. When this white paper speaks of the same rules at different scales or in different substrates, it means rules of this family.

Nor does the construction require finely tuned structure. Structural coordinates may start far from any useful configuration; the slow dynamics then move them until expected and observed correlations agree, so that the structure aligns itself with the statistics it is exposed to. How far this self-alignment can go is limited by the substrate, by the range, precision and timescales of the structural coordinates it offers. A physical system may thus obey the construction only weakly, with little headroom, and evolution and development can be read as processes that build substrates with more headroom (Sections [1.4](#sec-1-4) and [11](#sec-11)) {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`.

*Why "entropic".* The name refers to the dynamics, not only to their endpoint. A long tradition treats relative entropy as what drives motion, not just as a description of where a system ends up: fluxes driven by entropy gradients in irreversible thermodynamics {cite:p}`10.1103/physrev.38.2265`, the Fokker–Planck equation as a gradient flow of relative entropy {cite:p}`10.1137/s0036141096303359`, and the split of dynamics into a reversible, energy-driven part and an irreversible, entropy-driven part {cite:p}`10.1103/physreve.56.6620`, which mirrors the decomposition used in [Section 4.5](#sec-4-5). In this sense, inference in the units is a relaxation that lowers relative entropy towards the posterior, learning is constrained maximum entropy, and the solenoidal part of the dynamics leaves relative entropy unchanged. As with entropic forces, what drives all three is the multiplicity of hidden microstates, not a designed interaction. The programme of Entropic Dynamics {cite:p}`10.1088/1751-8113/44/22/225303` shares this intuition but not the construction.

## 4. From units to networks: entropic attractor networks

[Section 2](#sec-2) described the networks as they were first derived, from the FEP. [Section 3](#sec-3) described a single unit. This section puts the two together, and the fit is seamless: couple minimal units, and the networks of [Section 2](#sec-2) appear, with the same inference and learning rules {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`, now derived from stated physical assumptions rather than from the principle. Here the historical and the logical routes through this document meet ([Section 1.5](#sec-1-5)).

The result is a complete first-principles path, from the physics of a single unit to networks that infer and learn, and that do considerably more. Their attractors are Bayesian priors, which the learning rule self-orthogonalizes; sequences are stored in asymmetric couplings; old knowledge is protected while new knowledge is acquired; noise carries precision; and the same structure should reappear at coarser scales. None of these properties is added by design. Each follows from the collective dynamics when composing the units. [Section 4.1](#sec-4-1) shows how the unit slots into the network. The rest of the section takes the properties in turn: why the construction should hold across scales ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)), the emergent computational properties ([Section 4.3](#sec-4-3)), noise as information ([Section 4.4](#sec-4-4)), and multistability, metastability and action ([Section 4.5](#sec-4-5)).

(sec-4-1)=
### 4.1 How the unit fits in

Coupling minimal units needs no new ingredient. A unit's boundary, the coarse signal that drives its slow structural coordinates, now consists of the other units' states. The unit's fast inference becomes the node update, through the Langevin function L, and its slow learning becomes the rule for the couplings Wij (Jij in Spisak & Friston, 2026):

```{math}
\mathbb{E}_q[\sigma_i] = L\Big(b_i + \sum_{j \neq i} W_{ij}\,\sigma_j\Big), \qquad \Delta W_{ij} \propto \sigma_i\,\sigma_j - L\Big(b_i + \sum_{k \neq i} W_{ik}\,\sigma_k\Big)\,\sigma_j
```

Each term keeps the meaning that [Section 3](#sec-3) gave it. A node's prediction error, the difference between its state and its prediction, is the centred tangent of [Section 3.1](#sec-3-1). Each coupling Wij is the thermodynamic Legendre conjugate of the correlation between nodes i and j, which it adjusts until observed and predicted correlations agree ([Section 3.3](#sec-3-3)). The unit construction also fixes the timing of the rule: the prediction is formed from the preceding network state and paired with the renewed one. The rule belongs to the family of contrastive, local learning rules whose relation to inference is known for predictive-coding networks {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.2207.12316` and, in the symmetric equilibrium case, for Boltzmann machines {cite:p}`10.1207/s15516709cog0901_7`. The network itself is a stochastic Hopfield network generalized to non-equilibrium steady states, whose noisy dynamics sample from the global posterior.

*One-dimensional units, binary questions.* The minimality assumption makes every node, and therefore every attractor, one-dimensional: its continuous-Bernoulli state expresses graded evidence for or against a single event. This is why, with zero biases, the sign-inverse of every attractor is also a fixed point. An attractor and its mirror image are one prior, read as evidence for or against the same latent cause.

(sec-4-2)=
### 4.2 Across scales: one model family and the same landscape at every resolution

Both the generality of the approach and its application to fMRI rest on what survives coarse-graining: the form of the construction, and the attractor landscape it produces {raw:latex}`\bPrep{`{span .tag .tag-in-preparation}`In preparation`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`for brain data`{raw:latex}`}`.

*One family of forms.* Timescale separation is used twice: within the unit, where it separates perception from learning ([Section 3.3](#sec-3-3)), and between levels, where fast modes are enslaved by slow ones that carry the long-run dynamics {cite:p}`10.1007/978-3-642-88338-5; 10.1007/978-1-4612-5929-9; 10.1098/rsta.2019.0041; 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118243`. In whole-brain models, this has been proposed, and demonstrated in simulations, as the mechanism by which low-dimensional structure emerges {cite:p}`10.1038/s41540-025-00499-w`. If a coarse unit, such as a column or a brain region, exposes one macro-observable, a conjugate field acting on its transition ratios again yields an exponential family, with a response function and a susceptibility equal to its variance. The particular reference family need not survive averaging, but the architecture does: one exposed statistic, a conjugate field, fluctuation–dissipation, and a Bayesian reading when the macrostate represents an external cause. What changes with substrate and scale is which member of the family applies ([Section 3.4](#sec-3-4)). Single neurons, cortical columns and brain regions are therefore not expected to obey identical equations, but rules of one family, which share its signatures. Such rules may thus apply from single neurons, where the learning rule resembles spike-timing-dependent plasticity {cite:p}`10.1523/jneurosci.18-24-10464.1998` {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`, to brain regions (criterion D2, [Section 10](#sec-10)). Making this rigorous for networks is a goal of Line A (A2).

*The same landscape.* In fMRI network analyses, a coarser brain parcellation does not create a new network. It reports the activity of one neural system in fewer coordinates, the parcel means y = Cσ. It is a "camera", and different atlases are different cameras pointed at the same scene. The attractors pμ of the fine system are whole-brain patterns. Writing each as its regional profile aμ = Cpμ plus a within-parcel residual rμ, the one essential assumption is homogeneity: on the attractors that matter, units within a parcel participate alike, which is exactly what functional parcellations are designed for {cite:p}`10.1093/cercor/bhu239; 10.1038/s41583-018-0071-7`:

```{math}
p^{\mu}_{i} = a^{\mu}_{\alpha(i)} + r^{\mu}_{i}, \qquad \|\mathbf{r}^{\mu}\| \ll \|\mathbf{p}^{\mu}\|
```

Because the residuals average to zero within each parcel, fine inner products split exactly into a size-weighted coarse inner product and a residual term that homogeneity makes small:

```{math}
\langle \mathbf{p}^{\mu}, \mathbf{p}^{\nu} \rangle = \sum_{\alpha} |P_{\alpha}|\, a^{\mu}_{\alpha}\, a^{\nu}_{\alpha} + \langle \mathbf{r}^{\mu}, \mathbf{r}^{\nu} \rangle
```

Three consequences follow. First, attractors that differ across parcels remain visible, those that differ only within parcels merge, and nothing new should appear. In nested atlases {cite:p}`10.12688/mniopenres.12767.2`, higher resolution splits coarse attractors into descendants that average back to them. Second, approximate orthogonality at the fine scale is inherited at the coarse scale, in the size-weighted parcel metric (analyses in unweighted or z-scored coordinates therefore use size-balanced atlases). Third, only a functional camera is useful: spatially scattered random parcels average structured patterns away, shrinking them roughly with the square root of parcel size until at most a global mode remains, whereas contiguous random parcels degrade gradually, the finest attractors first. The claim is not that a network can be fitted at any scale, but that meaningful coarse-grainings preserve the landscape and meaningless ones erase it {cite:p}`10.1103/physrevlett.123.178103; 10.1162/netn_a_00175; 10.1126/science.1238723; 10.1063/1.4923066`.

*Assumptions, limits and tests.* Besides homogeneity, detecting the landscape with fitted networks assumes that the slow dynamics are organized by a modest number of attractors, and that the coarse network models the observed regional process. Orthogonality is a prediction (P1, P2), not a prerequisite. Nothing assumes that a parcel is a large neuron, that couplings survive block averaging, or that coarse-graining is an exact renormalization map. After haemodynamic deconvolution, fMRI is a slow camera for slow patterns {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neuroimage.2014.05.056; 10.1162/netn_a_00041`. Averaging cannot recover distinctions it has discarded, and residual haemodynamic mixing can mimic mixtures of successive states, which simulations must rule out before such mixtures are read as prior mixing (P10). Nested atlases in population and precision fMRI {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neuron.2017.07.011` let us test this directly, in five steps. First, we fit networks at a fine and at a coarse resolution, and check that the coarse attractors are the fine ones seen through the coarser atlas. Second, we check that, at finer resolution, coarse attractors split into sub-patterns rather than being replaced by new ones. Third, we measure how uniformly each attractor is expressed within parcels. Fourth, we test whether the near-orthogonality of the attractors carries over from the fine to the coarse scale, against null models of matched size. Fifth, we repeat the analysis with random parcellations, which should erase the landscape rather than preserve it. The range of resolutions is fixed in advance by test–retest reliability, and the matching is also done within single participants (B1; P18–P20).

### 4.3 Emergent properties

Once the units are coupled and learning, the network begins to show capacities that no single unit has. None of them is built into the units or written into the learning rule. They appear only when many units, each following the same local rules, interact. They also build on one another. These emergent properties are the bread and butter of the framework. Most of them were first described by {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`, and they are what makes the whole programme viable: if the construction is right, such properties should be visible in biological systems, and they may guide the development of more brain-like artificial ones. We collect and discuss them below ([Figure 5](#fig-emergent)), each tagged with its current status.

```{figure} figures/emergent.png

***Emergent properties of entropic attractor networks.*** ***A*** *Bayesian inference. Ongoing activity combines the current input, which enters as biases, with the global priors stored as attractors. The network's stochastic states are samples from the resulting posterior (Markov chain Monte Carlo), so local inference at the nodes adds up to Bayesian inference at the scale of the whole network.* ***B*** *Self-orthogonalization. The predictive-coding rule does not store a new stimulus as it is, but as its prediction error (PE): the residual relative to what the existing memory (A) already predicts. The new memory (B) is therefore approximately orthogonal to the old one.* ***C*** *Attractor–prior duality. With approximately orthogonal attractors, the couplings of the attractor network (left) can be written as a sum over attractors,* {math}`J_{ik} = \sum_j \beta_j W_{ij} W_{kj}`*. The network is then equivalent to one in which the attractors are explicit "virtual nodes" (right) that pool evidence from many regions and feed the result back, the Hopfield network–restricted Boltzmann machine equivalence (Barra et al., 2012).*
```

- **Attractors as priors** {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`. Attractor states store the network's priors, and ongoing activity combines them with current input into a posterior (Figures [2C](#fig-rules) and [5A](#fig-emergent)). Local inference at the nodes gives rise to inference at the level of the whole network.
- **Self-orthogonalization, the key property** {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`theory, in-silico demonstrations, first evidence in human fMRI`{raw:latex}`}`. Even for correlated inputs, the predictive-coding rule drives attractors towards approximate orthogonality: a new pattern is stored as its residual from what is already known (Figures [2B](#fig-rules) and [5B](#fig-emergent)). The network thereby approximates a Kanter–Sompolinsky projector network {cite:p}`10.1103/physreva.34.4217; 10.1103/physreva.35.380`, whose attractors coincide with the leading eigenvectors of the coupling matrix, but with a local learning rule. Offline routes towards similar couplings are known as well: Hebbian unlearning {cite:p}`10.1038/304158a0` and "dreaming", which provably drives Hebbian couplings towards the projector {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2019.01.006` and can exploit correlations in the data {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2025.107216`. In effect, self-orthogonalization is an on-line, approximate principal component analysis, in the tradition of Hebbian and anti-Hebbian subspace learning {cite:p}`10.1007/bf00275687; 10.1007/bf02331346; 10.1109/msp.2019.2933846`. Orthogonal attractors can act towards separating latent causes, much as in latent-cause models of learning ({cite:t}`10.1037/a0017808`; applied to extinction in [Section 6](#sec-6)) and use storage capacity efficiently. They also serve as a basis for the properties discussed below.
- **Attractor–prior duality** {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`Symmetric case: formally established equivalence; antisymmetric extension`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. This is the Hopfield network–restricted Boltzmann machine equivalence {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2012.06.003` in disguise. Approximate orthogonality makes the network statistically equivalent to a two-layer network in which the attractors are actual hidden nodes, connected only to the original nodes ([Figure 5C](#fig-emergent)). Our heuristic is that, with an antisymmetric coupling component, non-equilibrium steady states and solenoidal dynamics appear as within-layer connections between the attractor nodes. The appeal is direct: attractors act as integrator nodes that pool evidence from many regions and feed the result back, performing (Bayesian) information integration. A change in a single connection can then be decomposed into changes in its contribution to individual attractors.
- **Superposition and prior mixing** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`observed in simulations`{raw:latex}`}`. Linear combinations of stored priors can exist as attractors or as "ghost attractors", regions of slow dynamics without a true minimum {cite:p}`10.1523/jneurosci.2523-11.2012`. Such slow regions can do real work: in classical and dense associative memories, patterns remain transiently retrievable above capacity from the slow remnants of their former basins {cite:p}`10.1103/42y2-bsh1`. Unlike the spurious states of classical Hopfield networks {cite:p}`10.1103/physreva.32.1007`, they are useful here: the network can span the whole subspace of learned patterns and generalize to new inputs. Dense associative memories can likewise combine exponential capacity with emergent, useful minima that are not stored patterns {cite:p}`10.52202/085713-3947`. Mixing can also unfold in time, as quasi-periodic alternation across several attractors whose time-averaged posterior matches the data. Self-orthogonalization is what makes this work, and it offers a model for oscillatory brain dynamics {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005186; 10.1038/s41593-020-0671-1`.
- **Self-solenoidization** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`observed in simulations`{raw:latex}`}`. With our emergent learning rule, sequential presentation yields asymmetric couplings ({cite:t}`10.1103/physrevlett.57.2861; 10.1073/pnas.83.24.9469`; [Figure 2D](#fig-rules)). During transitions, the landscape set by the symmetric couplings tends to absorb the flow components along the free-energy gradient, via a mechanism closely related to self-orthogonalization. What remains is encoded mainly in the antisymmetric couplings, as a transversal flow along the free-energy isocontours: approximately solenoidal flow that replays sequences without changing the steady state (transversality derivation is in progress, full solenoidality is only a conjecture). Self-solenoidization would thus be an on-line, approximate Helmholtz decomposition, as self-orthogonalization is an on-line, approximate principal component analysis. It is the learned counterpart of potential–flux decompositions of non-equilibrium dynamics {cite:p}`10.1088/0305-4470/37/3/l01; 10.1073/pnas.0506347102; 10.1073/pnas.0800579105; 10.1073/pnas.1310692110`. Changing only the antisymmetric component may allow new action policies without altering the perceptual model ([Section 4.5](#sec-4-5)).
- **Continual learning** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`initial demonstration`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`. Two processes support it. First, stochastic multistability causes spontaneous replay of stored patterns, especially but not only in sequence dynamics ([Figure 2D](#fig-rules)), which consolidates old memories, as hippocampal and human replay is thought to do {cite:p}`10.1126/science.8036517; 10.1016/j.cell.2019.06.012` and as generative replay does in machine learning {cite:p}`10.48550/arXiv.1705.08690; 10.1038/s41467-020-17866-2`. Second, self-orthogonalization stores new information only as its residual relative to what is known, so old knowledge is preserved as long as it remains at least mildly useful. This parallels orthogonal-subspace methods in machine learning {cite:p}`10.1038/s42256-019-0080-x` and orthogonal task representations in brains and networks {cite:p}`10.1038/s41593-021-00821-9; 10.1016/j.neuron.2022.12.004`, as well as attractor scaffolds in hippocampal models whose memory degrades gracefully rather than collapsing {cite:p}`10.1038/s41586-024-08392-y`. Together they give resistance to catastrophic forgetting {cite:p}`10.1016/s0079-7421(08)60536-8; 10.1016/s1364-6613(99)01294-2` without task labels or explicit regularization {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.1611835114`. The same two processes should matter on long-horizon tasks, where a system has to keep learning while it acts, without eroding what it learned earlier in the task ([Section 1.1](#sec-1-1); P23).

(sec-4-4)=
### 4.4 Noise as information

In these networks, noise is not a nuisance to be suppressed. Its size encodes the precision of what a unit represents {raw:latex}`\bPrep{`{span .tag .tag-in-preparation}`In preparation`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`partly`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`.

Each unit's fluctuations sample its posterior, so their variance is the posterior variance and, by the fluctuation–dissipation relation of the exponential family {cite:p}`10.1088/0034-4885/29/1/306`, also the unit's susceptibility, or gain. A unit is therefore most responsive where it is most uncertain, and least responsive where it is saturated and certain: uncertainty is physically available to the system twice, as the size of its fluctuations and as its gain.

In information-geometric terms, this quantity is the Fisher metric {cite:p}`10.1162/089976698300017746; 10.1007/978-4-431-55978-8`, in which prediction-error tangents are measured. Precision weighting is thus simply the geometry of that tangent space: the metric supplies the precision weighting that predictive coding requires {cite:p}`10.3389/fnhum.2010.00215`, without a separate mechanism for estimating precision. At the network level, the metric is shaped by learning, through the couplings, and it separates the gradient and rotational parts of the dynamics ([Section 4.5](#sec-4-5)). Because finite samples produce exactly this kind of noise, a system that learns from finite experience represents its uncertainty without extra machinery.

This view connects to sampling-based accounts in which neural variability represents uncertainty {cite:p}`hoyer2003; 10.1016/j.tics.2010.01.003; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002211; 10.1016/j.neuron.2016.09.038`, to evidence that gain variability tracks uncertainty in visual cortex {cite:p}`10.1038/s41467-020-15533-0`, and to work on the sources and benefits of noise in neural systems {cite:p}`10.1038/nrn2258; 10.1038/nrn3061`. It is also what makes the framework a natural fit for stochastic and thermodynamic computing, which treat physical noise as a resource rather than an error {cite:p}`10.1063/1.5055860; 10.1109/icrc60800.2023.10386858; 10.1038/s41467-025-59011-x`.

(sec-4-5)=
### 4.5 Multistability, metastability and intrinsic action

Two dynamical properties of the networks matter both for brain dynamics and for action. Multistability comes from the attractors, and metastability from what the network learns about the transitions between them.

*Multistability* {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}`. Multiple attractors make the networks multistable, as theory, simulations and our brain models consistently show. Noise is what makes this multistability dynamic: rather than settling in one attractor, the system visits several over time. Such noise-driven exploration of multiple attractors has been proposed as a mechanism of resting-state brain dynamics {cite:p}`10.1038/nrn2961; 10.1523/jneurosci.2523-11.2012`. A deformable attractor manifold has recently been fitted to resting-state fMRI in the same spirit {cite:p}`10.64898/2025.12.02.691788`, and the geometry of dynamics on a fitted landscape has been related to attention ({cite:t}`10.1038/s41467-026-69041-8`; see [Section 1.3](#sec-1-3)).

*Metastability* {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`observed in simulations, see Figure 2D`{raw:latex}`}`. Sequence learning produces asymmetric couplings and a non-equilibrium steady state, with transient dwelling near attractors and directed transitions between them {cite:p}`kelso1995; 10.1126/science.1155564; 10.1016/j.neuron.2013.12.022; 10.1038/s41583-024-00883-1`. Because of self-orthogonalization, we expect these dynamics to be dominated by approximately solenoidal flow over the scalar free-energy landscape (self-solenoidization, [Section 4.3](#sec-4-3)). {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472` give a heuristic explanation of why this should happen; a rigorous derivation is still to be done. Broken detailed balance in human brain dynamics {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.2109889118; 10.1016/j.tics.2024.03.009` is consistent with this picture, and the non-equilibrium thermodynamics of networks with asymmetric couplings can be characterized analytically in kinetic Ising models {cite:p}`10.1038/s41467-023-39107-y`. In cortex, expectation modulates metastable attractor dynamics and speeds up coding {cite:p}`10.1038/s41593-019-0364-9`, close to the expectation-as-prior account tested in P6.

*Intrinsic action: paths over a learned world model* {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. The intuition is that the network learns paths over a world model it has already learned. Variational free energy (VFE) describes perception on the scalar landscape. A related quantity, possibly the expected free energy (EFE) of active inference {cite:p}`10.1080/17588928.2015.1020053; 10.7551/mitpress/12441.001.0001`, would describe intrinsic action, carried by the antisymmetric couplings between pairs of nodes. These couplings induce quasi-solenoidal dynamics: paths along isocontours of the landscape that determine the transitions. Without input, the same dynamics would produce spontaneous fluctuations at rest, or something like dreaming {cite:p}`10.1016/j.pneurobio.2012.05.003`, as in the spontaneous replay of {cite:t}`10.1016/j.neucom.2026.133472`. Whether the standard EFE arguments carry over to these networks is an open question {cite:p}`10.1162/neco_a_01354`.

*Decisions and planning* {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Learned transition paths could support decisions based on learned data, and planning as inference {cite:p}`attias2003; 10.1016/j.tics.2012.08.006`, especially across multiple timescales {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000209`, in the spirit of predictive maps and replay-based planning {cite:p}`10.1038/nature12112; 10.1038/nn.4650`. This has a continual-learning side too: the perceptual model, or world model, need not be relearned for every new task. Only the transition paths across its attractors change.

*Asymmetry, rotation and directed flow* {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Linearizing the mean-field dynamics around a fixed point separates, in the Fisher metric of [Section 4.4](#sec-4-4), a gradient-like part carried by the symmetric couplings from a rotational part carried by the antisymmetric ones. When asymmetry dominates, the local dynamics contain damped rotations, which links asymmetry directly to oscillation. Asymmetry is thus the minimal coupling structure that distinguishes temporal consequence from static correlation, and it turns an attractor landscape into directed sequence dynamics. In the dual picture ([Section 4.3](#sec-4-3)), attractors act as hidden nodes, and learned sequences live in directed couplings between them. If region-to-region asymmetries mainly serve these transitions, the antisymmetric couplings are low-rank and confined to the attractor subspace: the N(N − 1)/2 free asymmetries between region pairs reduce to about m(m − 1)/2 flows between m attractors. Such flows can leave the stationary density of the landscape unchanged, that is, be solenoidal, even though the attractors differ in depth, provided that each transition is weighted by the precision of the attractor it targets, or that the transitions form closed cycles through three or more attractors. Both routes lead to the same testable signature: attractor occupancies set by the symmetric landscape alone, with asymmetric currents that leave them unchanged. Finding this would itself be evidence of approximate self-solenoidization (P9). The derivations are sketched in *Technical sketch: asymmetry, rotation and solenoidal flow*.

*Gradient and rotation.* The mean-field dynamics and their linearization around a fixed point make the split between gradient and rotation explicit:

```{math}
\dot\theta_i = -\gamma\Big[\theta_i - b_i - \sum_j W_{ij}\,L_j(\theta_j)\Big], \qquad \delta\dot{\boldsymbol\theta} = M\,\delta\boldsymbol\theta, \qquad M = -\gamma\,(I - W D), \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}\big(\psi_1''(\theta_1), \ldots, \psi_n''(\theta_n)\big)
```

Here D holds each unit's susceptibility, which is also its variance and its Fisher information ([Section 4.4](#sec-4-4)). Splitting the couplings into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, W = S + A, and lowering an index with the Fisher metric separates a symmetric, gradient-like term from an antisymmetric, rotational one:

```{math}
D M = -\gamma\,(D - D S D) + \gamma\, D A D
```

For two units with J12 = S + A and J21 = S − A, the eigenvalues are −γ ± γ√((S² − A²) ψ1″ ψ2″). When |A| > |S|, the local dynamics contain a damped rotation with frequency ω = γ√((A² − S²) ψ1″ ψ2″), which links asymmetry directly to oscillation. Symmetric couplings can admit a reversible, equilibrium model, whereas antisymmetric couplings generically obstruct that interpretation and support directed probability currents.

*Solenoidal flow through the attractors.* With the m attractor patterns as the columns of Ξ, the N(N − 1)/2 free asymmetries between region pairs reduce to a small matrix Ω of attractor-to-attractor flows, with about m(m − 1)/2 free entries. With Λ holding the precision (curvature) of each basin, a linear–Gaussian sketch with isotropic noise gives the condition under which this flow is solenoidal, that is, leaves the stationary density of the landscape unchanged {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.0506347102`:

```{math}
H = \Xi\,\Lambda\,\Xi^{\top}, \qquad T = \Xi\,\Omega\,\Xi^{\top}, \qquad \text{solenoidal} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \Omega = Q\,\Lambda, \quad Q^{\top} = -Q
```

Exact solenoidality therefore does not require attractors of equal depth, which the present models do not have. It requires each directed coupling to be weighted by the precision of the attractor it targets, Ωμν = Qμν λν: an evidence weighting of the transitions. Besides their antisymmetric part, the transition couplings then carry a small symmetric part, ½ Qμν (λν − λμ), which grows with the difference in depth. Whether the predictive-coding learning rule produces this weighting by itself, since it subtracts precision-weighted predictions, is open, and simulations can measure it directly. A second route appears at the level of metastable hopping between attractors. There, added probability currents leave the occupancies unchanged exactly when they decompose into closed cycles, each added transition rate being scaled by the inverse occupancy of its source {cite:p}`10.1103/revmodphys.48.571`. A directed flow between just two attractors cannot preserve occupancies, but a cycle through three or more can, whatever their depths.

*An opportunity for directed connectivity from fMRI* {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Estimating directed connectivity between single pairs of regions from fMRI is notoriously hard, whether with dynamic causal modelling, Granger causality or related methods {cite:p}`10.1016/s1053-8119(03)00202-7; 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2010.08.063; 10.1523/jneurosci.4399-14.2015; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004762`, and the antisymmetric component is typically weak compared with the symmetric one. If asymmetries serve attractor transitions, estimating them at the attractor level pools evidence from many region pairs into a few attractor-to-attractor flows, which should improve statistical power; where needed, the flows can be projected back onto region pairs (P22). This applies the logic of the sufficiency test ([Section 5.2](#sec-5-2)) to directed structure, and it fits the coarse-grained state spaces in which broken detailed balance has been detected in human brain dynamics {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.2109889118`. Region-specific haemodynamic delays can create spurious asymmetries that do not average out at the attractor level; deconvolution, simulations with region-specific haemodynamic responses, and sequence tasks with a known order serve as controls.

## 5. Attractors as levels of description

*From the observer to the system.* In [Section 4.2](#sec-4-2), coarse-graining was done by an observer, who chose the slow observables. Collective dynamics suggest another source: attractors are, in effect, how a system coarse-grains itself, creating new, slow, macroscopic variables from within.

(sec-5-1)=
### 5.1 Attractors are not epiphenomena

A common objection to attractor accounts of brain function is that attractors are epiphenomena: convenient summaries of the dynamics, while the causal work is done by neurons and synapses alone. In this programme they are instead how new timescales, and with them new levels of description, come into being {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`builds on`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\bPub{`{span .tag .tag-published}`Published`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`results`{raw:latex}`}`. Pressed to its end, the objection becomes a philosophical question about causation across levels of description {cite:p}`10.7551/mitpress/4629.001.0001`, which we do not claim to settle. The programme does, however, offer several concrete reasons for the opposite view, each of which can be tested:

- **Attractors create timescales.** An attractor is where the dynamics slow down: the system typically dwells near it far longer than any of its units takes to relax. It is therefore a new, slower variable that the system creates for itself, and this is exactly the kind of timescale separation from which [Section 3](#sec-3) derives perception and learning. The state of an attractor network can then act as a slowly varying field, or prior, for faster processes, so that a construction of the same kind can repeat one level up (A2, [Section 8.1](#sec-8-1)). Attractors are thus not a by-product of inference and learning. They are how a system produces the separation of timescales on which its inference and learning rest. Hierarchies of intrinsic timescales across the cortex are consistent with this view {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000209; 10.1038/nn.3862`, as is the self-organization of functional hierarchies in recurrent networks with multiple timescales {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000220`.
- **Attractors do computational work.** They can store priors and separate latent causes through self-orthogonalization; they may bridge delays in trace conditioning and working memory; and, through their transitions, they can carry sequences and intrinsic action (Sections [4.3](#sec-4-3)–[4.5](#sec-4-5) and [6](#sec-6)). With symmetric couplings and approximately orthogonal attractors, the network is statistically equivalent to one in which the attractors are explicit hidden nodes that pool evidence and feed it back {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neunet.2012.06.003` (see [Section 4.3](#sec-4-3)). A description in which attractors are variables is then not a metaphor but an equivalent model.
- **Attractors make a difference under intervention.** On the interventionist account of causation {cite:p}`10.1093/0195155270.001.0001`, a variable is a cause if intervening on it changes what follows. Perturbation experiments reveal attractor dynamics in exactly this sense. An activity bump imposed optogenetically on the fly's heading circuit is maintained by the circuit {cite:p}`10.1126/science.aal4835`. Persistent activity in mouse frontal cortex returns to discrete attractor states after transient perturbations, or occasionally switches to the other attractor, followed by a switch in the animal's action {cite:p}`10.1038/s41586-019-0919-7`, and attractor dynamics gate the influence of distracting input on decisions {cite:p}`10.1038/s41593-021-00840-6`. The programme's own predictions are stated at the attractor level (extinction adds an attractor, chronic pain deepens one, expectations shift the posterior between them; P5, P6, P8) and can be probed with current neuroscience techniques, e.g. in closed loop with real-time fMRI (B6).
- **Attractors can form closed levels.** A macroscopic level is self-contained if it predicts its own future as well as the full microscopic state does (informational closure), and if interventions on macroscopic variables suffice to determine macroscopic outcomes (causal closure). {cite:t}`10.48550/arXiv.2402.09090` formalize such levels, which they liken to software running on physical hardware, and show that in a Hopfield network the overlaps with the stored patterns form a closed level of this kind: memory retrieval is computed at the level of the attractors, independently of microscopic detail {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.1314922110; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008289`. Timescale separation is what makes slow variables approximately closed: in synergetics, slow order parameters are generated by the fast variables and in turn enslave them, a circular causality in which the macro level is anything but idle {cite:p}`10.1007/978-3-642-88338-5; kelso1995`. [Section 4.2](#sec-4-2) adds that the inferential form survives coarse-graining, so a closed level can itself infer and learn. Whether attractor-level descriptions of brain dynamics are closed in this sense is an empirical question (P17). If it is, closure has a practical corollary: for whatever is computed at that level, the attractor state is a sufficient statistic, which turns closure into a test of what is computed in the attractors ([Section 5.2](#sec-5-2); P21).

{cite:t}`simon1962` argued that complex systems evolve far more readily when they can build on stable intermediate forms, and that the result is a nearly decomposable hierarchy, in which fast interactions within subsystems are separated from slow, aggregate interactions between them. Attractors are dynamical counterparts of such stable intermediate forms. Where levels are approximately closed in this sense, each can be studied largely in its own terms, without simulating everything beneath it, and an elementary step that recurs at each level needs to be understood only once ([Section 11](#sec-11)).

### 5.2 What is computed in the attractors? Counterfactuals and a sufficiency test

For brain data, the programme aims at mechanism, not at predictive accuracy. Two kinds of test follow: counterfactual simulation, and a test, derived from closure, of which quantities are carried by large-scale attractors and which are computed below them {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`proposed method`{raw:latex}`}`.

*Mechanism, not accuracy.* Data-driven predictive models of brain and behaviour are optimized to predict, and a mechanistic model need not beat them at it. Its value lies in answering questions they cannot answer: what would happen under an intervention that is absent from the training data. The fitted networks are generative and can be manipulated in silico: an attractor can be deepened or flattened, prior precision raised or lowered, the biases of selected regions shifted, or couplings altered. The decisive test is whether such counterfactual simulations, specified in advance, predict the effects of real manipulations, such as placebo and nocebo instructions, conditioning and extinction, pharmacological challenge, non-invasive brain stimulation or closed-loop real-time fMRI. The comparison is with mechanistically different variants of the same model (couplings fixed, biases only, a landscape without attractor structure), not with black-box predictors. Conventional predictive models keep an important role, however: as shown next, they set the reference that an attractor-level description has to reach, not beat.

*Two levels in one fit.* Score matching separates two components of the regional dynamics (graphical abstract, Line B). The couplings define the landscape and its attractors: dynamics at the network level. The biases capture what drives each region from below and from outside: mesoscale computation within regions, and sensory input. Both can be read out as time series from the same data: the attractor state a(t), given by the overlaps of the regional state x(t) with the fitted attractors (or by the attractor towards which each state relaxes), and the input b(t) inferred by the fitted model.

*Closure implies sufficiency.* If the attractor level is closed in the sense of [Section 5.1](#sec-5-1) (P17), and a quantity y (a perceptual report, a behavioural choice, an experimental condition or a clinical variable) is computed at that level, then the regional data carry no information about y beyond what the attractor state carries. The attractor state is then a sufficient statistic for y:

```{math}
p\big(y \mid x\big) = p\big(y \mid a(x)\big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I(y; x) = I(y; a)
```

Operationally, y should be predictable from the attractor time series just as well as from the full regional data: not better, but without loss. Because a(t) and b(t) are both computed from the regional time series by the fitted model, the chain rule of mutual information splits what the data carry about y exactly into three parts:

```{math}
I(y; x) = \underbrace{I(y; a)}_{\text{attractor level}} + \underbrace{I(y; b \mid a)}_{\text{upstream input}} + \underbrace{I(y; x \mid a, b)}_{\text{neither}}
```

Quantities for which the first term exhausts the total are computed in attractor space. Those that need the second term are carried by upstream, mesoscale processing or by sensory drive. A substantial third term points to structure that the model misses. In this way closure, a property of a level, becomes a criterion for what lives on that level.

*Mixed cases and controls.* Many brain functions will be mixed, and the attractor share I(y; a)/I(y; x) is then a graded measure rather than a verdict. Its value is that it is principled rather than descriptive: the criterion follows from the theory, not from choosing whichever feature set predicts best. Three controls keep it honest. First, sufficiency must be specific: equally compact coarse-grainings that ignore the attractors (principal components, random projections, canonical networks of the same dimension) should lose information that the attractor coordinates keep; otherwise the result reflects compression in general rather than attractors. Second, the information terms are estimated from the cross-validated performance of nested models fitted with the same learner, and are bounded in practice by sample size, model capacity and haemodynamic blurring. Equal performance is therefore established with equivalence tests, not read off a non-significant difference. Third, sufficiency for an external quantity is necessary but not sufficient for closure, which also requires the attractor level to predict its own future (P17), so the two tests are run together. The same logic applies between resolutions: replacing x with data from a finer atlas, or at voxel level, asks whether the coarse attractors suffice for what the finer data carry ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)).

*An example: pain.* Pain is a natural test case because it combines strong input with strong expectations. If expectations act as attractor priors (P6), placebo and nocebo effects on reported pain should be largely attractor-sufficient, and an in-silico shift of the corresponding prior should reproduce them. The effect of stimulus intensity, by contrast, should be carried mostly by the biases, as nociceptive drive. In chronic pain the same decomposition can be followed over time, to see whether chronification moves pain from an input-carried to an attractor-level phenomenon, or the reverse (P8).

## 6. Emergent, biology-like phenomena: hypotheses

Several well-known features of biological learning may follow from the same few properties, above all from self-orthogonalization. Below we highlight a few examples, each a hypothesis to be tested rather than a result. Further-reaching possibilities are collected in [Section 11](#sec-11).

- **Primacy effect** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Because new information is stored only as a residual relative to existing attractors, early material serves as the reference frame for what follows and should be remembered better. This would mirror the primacy effect in human memory {cite:p}`10.1037/h0045106`, in contrast to classical palimpsest networks, which forget their oldest memories first {cite:p}`10.1209/0295-5075/2/4/013; 10.1088/0305-4470/19/10/011`.
- **Extinction, renewal and reinstatement** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Self-orthogonalization restricts how representations can change:
  - *Acquisition.* A newly learned representation is stored in an attractor approximately orthogonal to all previous ones, so only its residual is stored, and old representations shift as well (remapping) {cite:p}`10.1016/j.tins.2008.06.008`.
  - *Context.* Overlap with old memories, for example an association with fear or pain, induces correlations that must be balanced by non-overlapping components to keep the attractors orthogonal. This is how context A becomes part of the memory.
  - *Extinction.* The old memory could only be erased if everything else stayed unchanged, which is practically impossible. Instead a new attractor forms, again orthogonal to the others. Because the stimulus is similar or identical but the outcome differs, self-orthogonalization puts strong pressure on using context to tell the two situations apart: context B.
  - *Renewal and reinstatement.* The old context, or the unconditioned stimulus itself, therefore brings back the original response. Through sequence learning, the network may even anticipate it.
  - *Related work.* This mechanistic account parallels the evidence that extinction is new, context-dependent learning {cite:p}`10.1037/0097-7403.1.1.88; 10.1101/lm.78804; 10.1101/lm.77504`, latent-cause models in which extinction creates a new latent cause {cite:p}`10.1037/a0017808; 10.7554/elife.23763`, state-splitting accounts of renewal {cite:p}`10.1037/0033-295x.114.3.784` and the neurobiology of fear extinction {cite:p}`10.1146/annurev.psych.121208.131631`.
- **Trace conditioning and working memory** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. Self-sustaining attractor activity that persists after the stimulus has gone would bridge the interval in trace conditioning {cite:p}`10.1126/science.280.5360.77` and support working memory, as in classical persistent-activity models {cite:p}`10.1016/0896-6273(95)90304-6; 10.1093/cercor/7.3.237; 10.1016/s0166-2236(00)01868-3`. Activity-silent accounts {cite:p}`10.1016/j.tics.2015.05.004` provide the contrast to test against.
- **Oscillations during acquisition** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. When a new stimulus is not well explained by any existing attractor, ghost attractor or superposition, only oscillatory sampling across attractors remains. This is slow and costly, because the boundaries between attractors have high free energy, so learning will try to store the new information in an attractor, or at least a ghost, while keeping orthogonality (remapping). The oscillations should then fade as learning proceeds. This may relate to theta-band activity during acquisition and encoding {cite:p}`10.1016/s0896-6273(02)00586-x; 10.1162/089976602317318965; 10.1126/science.1085818; 10.1038/nn.3582`, and to the oscillations that emerge in circuits optimized for sampling-based inference {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005186; 10.1038/s41593-020-0671-1`.
- **Curiosity and exploration** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}` {raw:latex}`\tagnote{`{span .tag-note}`open`{raw:latex}`}`. How far the usual active-inference arguments for epistemic value {cite:p}`10.1080/17588928.2015.1020053; 10.1162/neco_a_00999` carry over to these networks is unclear, but uncertainty here naturally encourages exploration {cite:p}`10.3389/neuro.12.006.2007; 10.1109/tamd.2010.2056368; 10.1038/s41583-018-0078-0`. In this picture a "dark room" {cite:p}`10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00130` is not predictable: it is a lack of evidence, which leaves spontaneous activity to sample the priors and may produce hallucination-like states. This links to accounts of hallucinations as strong priors {cite:p}`10.1126/science.aan3458; 10.1016/j.tics.2018.12.001` and to pain without input, as in phantom-limb pain {cite:p}`10.1038/nrn1991`.
- **Reinforcement learning** {raw:latex}`\bHyp{`{span .tag .tag-hypothesis}`Hypothesis`{raw:latex}`}`. The networks are compatible with reinforcement learning in the way active-inference accounts propose {cite:p}`10.1371/journal.pone.0006421; sutton2018`, but this relies on fixed priors set by evolution, such as pain. Pain research is therefore a good test bed {cite:p}`10.1016/j.neuron.2014.02.042; 10.1038/nrn3976; 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005142`.

## 7. Predictions and falsification criteria

Every prediction below is stated in advance, with the test bed and the result that would count against it. Self-orthogonalization is the system's most prominent signature, but it is hard to test in high dimensions: random vectors also tend towards orthogonality. Real inputs are almost always structured and correlated, so orthogonality of learned attractors is not trivial. Still, the decisive test we propose is the projector-network signature (P2), not orthogonality alone. All geometric tests use strong null models: phase-randomized time series, symmetry-preserving permutations, covariance-matched Gaussian models, and condition-label shuffling. Analysis plans will be preregistered (see the note at the start of [Section 8](#sec-8)). For the neuroscience predictions the criterion is mechanistic: counterfactual simulations specified in advance, and the sufficiency test of [Section 5.2](#sec-5-2), rather than predictive accuracy beyond that of existing data-driven models.

## 8. Technical plan

:class: note

This section is an overview. It will be extended by detailed appendices that serve as preregistrations of the methodology. Each will be released with its own DOI before the corresponding work begins, so that methods and analysis plans are fixed in advance and readers can check what was done against what was planned.

The three lines share one model class, one set of definitions and one set of predictions. Results from each line are checked against the others, and are often published together. Sharing one model also creates synergies that neither line could produce on its own ([Figure 6](#fig-lines)). The same Bayesian reading of the attractors, in which priors and inputs are combined according to their precision, applies to task fMRI in Line B and to artificial data, such as noisy faces, in Line C. A finding in one domain therefore translates directly into a hypothesis or a benchmark in the other: brain data show which regimes of prior and input precision biology uses, and machines test those regimes under full control.

(sec-8-1)=
### 8.1 Line A – Approach and theory

- **A1. Complete and test the unit-level construction.** Publish the derivation; test its contingent predictions in finite jump-process simulations in which no inference or learning rule is coded: the form of the nonlinearity under different resting laws, fluctuation–gain equality, and the dependence of orthogonalization on learning-rule timing (P3, P4).
- **A2. Composition and hierarchy.** Formalize how evidence at one level acts as the effective field at the next, and how nested timescales give rise to hierarchical message passing. Derive principled coarse-graining criteria: bounds on how well attractors are preserved as a function of within-parcel heterogeneity, and conditions under which the coarse transition law, not only the attractor set, is preserved ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)). Show how attractors at one level supply the slow timescale for the next, and characterize when the resulting levels are informationally and causally closed ([Section 5.1](#sec-5-1); P17).
- **A3. Action and planning.** Develop the network-level account of action: solenoidal flow from antisymmetric couplings as intrinsic action, and effector-driving nodes as extrinsic action. Study planning as sequence prediction over the learned landscape, and the link between learnable surprise and exploration.
- **A4. Thermodynamic cost.** Relate the quantities of the construction (alignment gap, learnable versus unlearnable surprise) to the heat dissipated during perception and learning, as a bridge to physical implementation. Because each synapse is the thermodynamic Legendre conjugate of a correlation, learning has a thermodynamic price that can be computed in theory and measured in hardware.

*Methods:* non-parametric information geometry, stochastic thermodynamics, averaging and adiabatic theory, and large-scale simulation.

(sec-8-2)=
### 8.2 Line B – Brains: generative models of large-scale brain dynamics

- **B1. Model fitting.** With the open-source package *attractome*, fit networks to resting-state, task and naturalistic fMRI by score matching with haemodynamic deconvolution, estimating couplings (network-level dynamics) and biases (upstream, mesoscale input). Validate generatively, by simulating held-out data, and against null models. This includes individual-level models from dense, repeatedly sampled single-subject datasets. Before interpretation, the fitted landscapes are tested across resolutions: project and match across nested atlases, measure homogeneity, and compare with random parcellations, in population and single-subject data ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2); P18–P20).
- **B2. Tests of the theory.** Attractor orthogonality and eigenmode alignment (P1, P2), the couplings–input decomposition (P7), self-solenoidization, transition learning and directed attractor-to-attractor flows in sequence tasks (P9, P13, P22), prior mixing and oscillatory alternation between attractors (P10, P15), spontaneous sampling of priors at rest (P16), and the closure of the attractor-level description (P17) and its corollary, attractor sufficiency (P21). Ultra-high-field (7T) laminar fMRI tests whether inferred priors and inputs map onto feedback and feedforward cortical layers.
- **B3. Learning without forgetting: extinction and renewal (P5).** Fit landscapes before and after acquisition, extinction and renewal in threat-learning paradigms, and test whether extinction adds a new, approximately orthogonal attractor.
- **B4. Expectations as priors (P6).** Model placebo and nocebo effects on pain as precision-weighted combinations of attractor priors and nociceptive input, using placebo-imaging consortium data. Test whether in-silico shifts of the prior, specified in advance, reproduce the observed effects, and whether placebo responses are attractor-sufficient while the effects of stimulus intensity are carried by the input ([Section 5.2](#sec-5-2)).
- **B5. Plasticity in pain chronification (P8).** Separate meso-scale (region-intrinsic) from macro-scale (network) plasticity in chronic low back pain, fibromyalgia and longitudinal phantom-limb pain cohorts, using the attractor–input decomposition to follow, within subjects, the level at which pain is carried as it becomes chronic (P8, P21).
- **B6. From predictive to mechanistic neuromarkers.** Brain-based predictive models of pain, cognition and clinical outcomes have matured: they can be replicable with moderate sample sizes {cite:p}`10.1038/s41586-023-05745-x`, and some generalize to independent patient populations {cite:p}`10.1038/s41467-019-13785-z`. Most, however, rest on data-agnostic machine learning, so their behaviour out of context is hard to predict, their features are hard to map onto mechanisms, and they cannot simulate what has not been measured. The aim here is not to outperform them but to explain what they capture. The sufficiency test ([Section 5.2](#sec-5-2)) asks whether the information a predictive model exploits is carried by large-scale attractors or by upstream input, with the predictive model itself as the reference to be matched. Compact model-based features (attractor depth, dwell times, transition costs) are validated externally across cohorts, and counterfactual simulation generates intervention hypotheses that are specified in advance. Real-time fMRI with reinforcement learning from brain feedback provides closed-loop, causal tests of these hypotheses.

*Data:* large open datasets and cohorts from established collaborations (placebo and pain consortia, two DFG collaborative research centres, an ERA-NET pain consortium).

(sec-8-3)=
### 8.3 Line C – Computers: emergent learning as an alternative route to AI

- **C1. Software and scaling.** Efficient, open implementations; map the networks' operational regimes (temperature, noise, input precision) and scaling behaviour.
- **C2. A neuroscience-inspired benchmark battery.** Continual learning, extinction, reinstatement and renewal, interference, primacy, reversal and sequence tasks, matched to the paradigms of Line B so that brain and machine are compared on the same tasks (P5, P11, P14), and long-horizon tasks scored against a repeated-sampling baseline (P23). Baselines: standard continual-learning methods (regularization- and replay-based) and backpropagation-trained recurrent networks.
- **C3. Uncertainty and generative inference.** Bayesian reconstruction and denoising as a function of prior and input precision; calibration of the networks' uncertainty; generalization through prior mixing (P10).
- **C4. Native physical implementation (P12).** Stochastic binary units are the tanh case of the minimal unit, which makes probabilistic-bit and related hardware a natural fit. Steps: simulate hardware-constrained networks; run inference natively on hardware with the local learning rule applied from the measured states; then co-design slowly adapting couplings native to the substrate. Candidate substrates: thermodynamic, memristive, nanomagnetic and related emerging architectures {cite:p}`10.1063/1.5055860; 10.1109/icrc60800.2023.10386858; 10.1038/s41928-022-00886-9`, with partners. Device evaluation has so far relied on emulation metrics that rate how faithfully a device reproduces biological synapses, neurons or networks {cite:p}`10.1038/s44287-026-00321-7`. As a complement, the construction suggests theory-derived figures of merit: the separation of timescales between state and conductance, a bounded, maximum-entropy response, noise variance that tracks gain, bidirectional local updates with the prescribed timing, support for asymmetric couplings, and, at the network level, attractor orthogonality and resistance to forgetting.
- **C5. Closed-loop agency.** Maze, reversal and detour tasks probe world-model formation, planning and autonomous action, and bridge back to behavioural neuroscience.

| Assumptions of the construction do not hold for a given substrate | The inferential reading is not guaranteed there | Delimits the scope of the theory; the network models remain usable as phenomenological generative models |
| Orthogonality in brain data is explained by high-dimensional chance or measurement artefacts | P1 and P2 fail | Strong null models detect this; attention shifts to dynamical predictions (P5–P9) |
| fMRI is too slow or too noisy for some signatures | Weak tests in Line B | Laminar 7T fMRI, dense single-subject sampling, and simulations with matched noise; electrophysiology via collaborators |
| Counterfactual simulations fail to predict the effects of real manipulations | The fitted landscapes describe brain dynamics without explaining them | Model variants with fixed couplings or biases locate which component fails; the sufficiency analysis still maps which quantities are carried at the attractor level; results are published either way |
| Networks do not scale on conventional hardware | Line C limited to small and medium problems | Focus on properties (continual learning, calibrated uncertainty) rather than scale; emerging hardware as the long-run route |
| Emerging hardware cannot realize slow adaptive couplings | Native learning (C4) not reached | Hardware-in-the-loop learning remains publishable; results inform hardware development |
| Brain and machine share no common signatures | The common-principle hypothesis fails | A clear negative result: the theory may describe a viable route to learning that brains do not use |
| Attractor-level descriptions of brain data are not closed (P17 fails) | Attractors may be better treated as descriptions than as a level in their own right | The fitted networks remain generative models of regional dynamics; closure is then tested at finer scales and in simulations, where timescale separation is larger (A2) |
| Few quantities are attractor-sufficient; most are mixed (P21) | No clean assignment of functions to levels | The attractor share remains a graded, theory-derived measure; a consistently large input share would itself show that large-scale attractors carry less of the computation than proposed |
| Attractors do not match across resolutions (P18 or P19 fails) | fMRI-fitted networks cannot be read as views of one landscape | Claims are restricted to resolutions validated by held-out generative tests; precision fMRI, 7T data and simulations with known ground truth locate where homogeneity fails |

### Limitations and open questions

Much of this programme rests on heuristics that still have to be made rigorous. We list the main ones so that readers can weigh them.

- **Sufficient, not necessary.** The unit-level construction shows one route from physics to inference and learning. It does not show that brains, or any particular material, take this route.
- **Strong assumptions.** Timescale separation, bounded coordinates and leading-order interactions are idealizations. Where they fail, the inferential reading is not guaranteed.
- **Heuristics not yet derived.** Self-solenoidization, the duality with antisymmetric couplings, prior mixing through oscillatory alternation, and the role of expected free energy in intrinsic action are hypotheses, not results.
- **Not an exact gradient.** The network learning rule is not the exact gradient of a single objective, so its stationary behaviour is characterized only approximately.
- **Minimal units.** One-dimensional, continuous-Bernoulli units are the simplest case; multidimensional or spiking units need extensions.
- **Measurement.** fMRI is slow, indirect and confounded by vascular and physiological signals. Results depend on coarse-graining choices, since a parcellation shows only distinctions that live across parcels ([Section 4.2](#sec-4-2)), and orthogonality in high dimensions needs strong null models.
- **Scale.** Demonstrations so far are small to medium in scale. We make no claim of competitiveness with large deep-learning systems.
- **Speculative links.** The broader view of life as stacked timescales and the connections to quantum-like geometry and to consciousness are collected in the Outlook ([Section 11](#sec-11)). The rest of the programme does not depend on them.
- **The roots, not the whole tree.** The programme aims to explain where the brain's capacities come from, not the full complexity with which a particular brain realizes them ([Section 1.4](#sec-1-4)). Success would leave most of neuroscience's hard problems open, while giving them, we hope, a common starting point.
- **Levels and causation.** Whether attractors are causes or descriptions ultimately touches philosophical questions that the programme does not settle {cite:p}`10.7551/mitpress/4629.001.0001`. We argue only for causal relevance in senses that can be tested: the creation of timescales, the effects of interventions, and closure ([Section 5.1](#sec-5-1); P17).

## 11. Outlook: further-reaching possibilities

The rest of the programme does not depend on the ideas in this section {raw:latex}`\bSpec{`{span .tag .tag-speculative}`Speculative`{raw:latex}`}`.

We state them because they follow naturally from the construction, and because stating them openly makes it easier to see where it might lead, or where it might break.

*Life as stacked timescales.* From the point of view of [Section 5](#sec-5), life stacks timescales, from molecules and cells to circuits, behaviour, development and evolution, and it appears to do so in a way that largely respects the boundaries between levels ([Section 5.1](#sec-5-1)). Hopfield-type attractor dynamics indeed recur across biology, from cell differentiation and epigenetic memory to molecular self-assembly {cite:p}`10.1146/annurev-biophys-101425-023356`; local adaptation of interactions can produce associative memory at the scale of a whole system {cite:p}`10.1162/artl_a_00029`; and evolution and development have been cast as learning across nested levels and timescales {cite:p}`10.1073/pnas.2120037119; 10.1177/26339137231168355`. That living systems largely respect these boundaries is a great gift for scientific understanding, and it is what makes the programme's bet plausible: if the same elementary step recurs at each level, understanding it once gives a handle on many.

*Evolution as refining a law rather than inventing rules.* If the elementary step is a property of organized matter rather than a biological invention (Sections [1.4](#sec-1-4) and [3.4](#sec-3-4)), evolution may have worked less like an engineer designing rules and more like a craftsman perfecting a material. It would start from structures that align only weakly with their environment and favour substrates in which the same law operates with more headroom: more reliable separation of timescales, wider ranges of coupling, richer architectures. Because better-aligned systems can in turn build better substrates, the gains could compound, so that alignment improves at an accelerating, perhaps exponential, rate over evolutionary time. This echoes views of evolution and development as one scale-free process acting on materials that are already competent {cite:p}`10.1002/bies.201900228; 10.1007/s00018-023-04790-z`, and of evolution as learning {cite:p}`10.1177/26339137231168355; 10.1073/pnas.2120037119`. Whether any of this can be made quantitative is open.

*Quantum-like geometry.* The rotational, solenoidal part of the dynamics ([Section 4.5](#sec-4-5)) leads into the territory of tangent and cotangent bundles. On a dually flat statistical manifold, Dombrowski's construction gives the tangent bundle a Kähler structure {cite:p}`10.1515/crll.1962.210.73; molitor2012; 10.1016/j.geomphys.2013.03.015`, the same kind of geometry that underlies the geometric formulation of quantum mechanics {cite:p}`10.1007/bf01225149; 10.1007/978-1-4612-1422-9_3`. Closed solenoidal loops may then accumulate geometric, Berry-like phases {cite:p}`10.1098/rspa.1984.0023`, which are known to arise in classical stochastic kinetics {cite:p}`10.1209/0295-5075/77/58001`. Whether this formal kinship can account for quantum-like phenomena in cognition and decision making {cite:p}`10.1017/cbo9780511997716; 10.1017/s0140525x12001525` is open.

*Consciousness.* Attractors offer a plausible latent dimension for a global workspace {cite:p}`baars1988; 10.1073/pnas.95.24.14529; 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.01.026; 10.1016/j.pneurobio.2020.101918` or for the "inner screen" of {cite:t}`10.31234/osf.io/6afs3`. We make no claim here beyond pointing out these links.
